Three manga directions

Manga is built on exactly the thing this product does: panels that reveal information in a controlled order, and screentone that hides detail in plain sight until you look closely. Each direction below takes a different register — investigation, comedy, impact — and each still has to survive the same test as the last round: how it handles the moment the product admits it cannot measure something.

What it gets right

  • The koma grid is genuine information design. Panel size encodes importance, so the layout does work rather than decoration.
  • The inked-out panel is the best answer yet to the honest limit. A black panel with no evidence in it reads instantly as "nothing here to show you" — not as a failure.
  • Survives long-form. Of the three, this is the only one that could still carry nine guides and a methodology page without exhausting the reader.

What it costs

  • Investigation framing cuts close to the surveillance mood the brand rules avoid — the copy has to stay helpful, not paranoid.
  • Black gutters eat space on small screens; the grid needs a real mobile collapse, not a scale-down.
  • Least quirky of the three. If quirk is the point, this is the conservative pick.

What it gets right

  • The strip does the explaining. Four panels convey the product faster than any hero paragraph, and it is the format people already know how to read.
  • Balloon shape encodes certainty. A jagged shout for a confirmed finding, a dashed wobble for the one we cannot check — the honesty is carried by form, not by a caveat nobody reads.
  • Unmistakably not a scam site. Nobody running a shady converter builds a mascot; the warmth is itself a trust signal.

What it costs

  • Hardest to keep serious. A privacy tool that jokes about your location leaking has to land the tone exactly, or it reads as making light of the risk.
  • The mascot needs a real illustrator eventually. CSS gets you this far; it will not carry a whole brand.
  • Weakest for the technical audience who links to you — needs the detailed readout kept somewhere behind it.

Shūchūsen 集中線 concentration lines

Shōnen impact. Focus lines converge on the moment of revelation, the signal count lands like a hit, and everything carries a hard offset shadow. The conceptual move is in what doesn’t get the treatment: the unverifiable row is deliberately flat, grey and shadowless — the one thing all that energy cannot touch.

What it gets right

  • The best idea across all six directions. Denying the unverifiable row the visual treatment everything else gets says "we cannot reach this" without a single word of copy.
  • The result count becomes the hero moment, which is exactly the beat that makes someone share a screenshot of your tool.
  • Cheapest to build well. Focus lines and offset shadows are a few lines of CSS, and it stays sharp at any screen size.

What it costs

  • Shouting is exhausting past the first screen. The guides would need a much calmer sibling treatment, which is effectively a second design.
  • High energy plus "watermark remover" risks the illicit read — the exact positioning the brand rules rule out.
  • Outlined italic display type is fragile in translation and at small sizes.

One thing to decide deliberately, whichever you pick

The Japanese here is accurate and deliberately never load-bearing — every Japanese string sits next to English that carries the same meaning. Keep that rule. It matters for three practical reasons: readers without a CJK font installed see empty boxes, screen readers will announce Japanese in an English page unless each run is tagged lang="ja", and search engines index an English page. Treat it as ornament with a translation attached, never as the only place a meaning lives.

Recommendation

Tantei, with Shūchūsen’s dead panel

Take the serious one, and steal the best idea from the loud one

Tantei is the only one of the three that can be the whole site. It reads as manga immediately — the koma grid, the black gutters, the tone — but it stays legible across nine long guides and a methodology page, which is where the traffic actually compounds. The investigation framing also fits what the product literally does: opens a file and reports what it finds.

Then take one idea from Shūchūsen: withholding the visual treatment from the thing you cannot measure. Tantei already does a version of this with the inked-out panel, and it is worth making the rule explicit across the whole design — confirmed findings get tone, weight and a spot colour; the unverifiable row gets none of them. That is your product principle expressed as a design system rule rather than a disclaimer.

Yonkoma I would keep, but not as the site. The four-panel strip is the best explainer you could put on the homepage or at the top of a guide, and the mascot is genuinely good marketing material. Use it as a component inside Tantei rather than as the whole identity.
